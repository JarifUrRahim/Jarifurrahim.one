Chapter 0 — The Awakening: From Classroom to Career Consulting

Source status: A book adaptation of the author’s article, The Awakening: My Journey from Classroom to Career Consulting. The central event and question are preserved; the structure is expanded for this manuscript.

Every serious journey has an origin that appears too small to deserve the name. It may be a question held quietly in a classroom, a discomfort with a familiar explanation, or a moment when a person notices the gap between what is said and what is lived.

My own question began while I was learning Computer Software Engineering. I heard that computer knowledge could create new possibilities: remote work, digital services, independent income, technical careers, and a different relationship to the future. The promise was compelling. It spoke to many young people who wanted an exit from limitation without leaving their place in the world.

But one question stayed with me.

If these possibilities are real, why is the path from computer knowledge to real-world capability so often invisible?

The question was not an accusation against teachers. Teaching deserves respect because it carries the responsibility of helping another human being see more clearly. My concern was with a recurring distance between description and application. A student can be told that a skill matters and still not know how to use it. They can hear that opportunity exists and still be unable to identify a first real problem they can solve.

That distance became the beginning of an awakening.

A question that asked for evidence

Many forms of education are built around transmission. A teacher knows something; a learner receives it; an examination measures part of what was retained. This can be valuable. It creates common language, introduces a field, and protects knowledge from being lost.

Yet transmission is not the same as transformation.

Transformation begins when a learner can use knowledge in an encounter with reality. They can recognise a need, choose a method, test an idea, receive criticism, revise an approach, and take responsibility for the outcome. Until then, knowledge may remain accurate and still remain distant.

The question from the classroom was therefore not only about income. It was about evidence. What does it look like when knowledge enters life? What proof should a learner seek before they build their future around a claim? How can guidance become honest enough to show both opportunity and difficulty?

These questions have followed every project in this book.

Respecting education while asking more of it

It is easy to become cynical when institutions do not meet every need. Cynicism is not the answer. It can turn a valid question into contempt, and contempt is too weak a foundation for building anything useful.

The better response is to preserve respect while increasing the standard. A teacher does not need to know every path a student may take. No one person can. But a responsible learning environment should make room for practice, uncertainty, lived examples, and the courage to say, “Here is what I know; here is what I am still learning; here is how you can test this for yourself.”

That kind of honesty does not weaken authority. It makes authority trustworthy.

It also changes the learner’s role. A learner stops waiting for a complete answer to be delivered and begins building a relationship with evidence. They create a small project. They ask someone using a skill in the real world what the work actually demands. They document an attempt. They learn to distinguish a polished promise from a workable process.

The goal is not to make every person an entrepreneur or a technologist. The goal is to make every person more capable of locating themselves inside a changing world.

From the classroom to the field

Later, when I had the opportunity to teach Computer Software Engineering as a Junior Instructor at Khanjahan Ali College of Science and Technology in Khulna, the early question returned in a different form. The classroom mattered. But it could not be the final destination.

What would it mean to help students turn learning into a living capability? How could a lesson on a tool become a lesson about identity, usefulness, ethics, and direction? How could someone move from the anxiety of “What will happen to me?” toward the more active question, “What can I learn to contribute?”

Rashik — The Awakening emerged from that tension. It was not conceived as a claim to possess every answer. It was an attempt to build a bridge: between digital awareness and practical skill; between career planning and moral orientation; between the speed of technology and the slower work of becoming responsible for one’s choices.

The bridge had to be practical. It had to involve real tools, portfolios, digital identity, communication, systems, and projects. But it also had to be human. A person who gains a tool without gaining judgment may become more efficient without becoming more free.

The changing form of the same question

At first, the question was about computer knowledge. Then it became a question about careers. Then it became a question about digital systems, AI, public writing, research, and the responsibilities that come with building things other people may use.

The form changed. The core did not.

Every tool creates a new version of the old question: can this knowledge be placed in a human life without reducing that life to the tool? Can a person become more capable without becoming less reflective? Can progress be measured not only by speed, income, or visibility, but by whether a system helps people carry their responsibilities with greater clarity?

This book does not claim that the answers are simple. It is a record of trying to ask them more carefully.

A beginning that remains unfinished

Chapter 0 is deliberately called a beginning, not an origin story with a finished moral. The story continues because the question continues. Every new platform, project, and article tested the same premise: knowledge should not remain a performance of knowledge. It should become a form of service, evidence, and responsible action.

The reader is not asked to accept this premise immediately. They are asked to examine their own life for the moment when a gap between information and reality became impossible to ignore. Perhaps it occurred in a class, a workplace, a family decision, or an encounter with technology.

That moment may not provide an answer. It can still provide a direction.

And direction is often enough to begin.

Reader test

Write down one piece of advice you have received about your future. Then ask: what would count as evidence that this advice fits my actual life? The answer may reveal the first small project you need to attempt.
